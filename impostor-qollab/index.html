IMPOSTOR

Can you fake a quantum computer?

In April 2026 someone won 1 BTC for “breaking” a key on a quantum computer. Then someone swapped the quantum computer for /dev/urandom, and the key still came out. So how do you know something is quantum? Here you play the faker. Use any classical trick you like. A referee written in Qiskit will catch you, and show you the theorem that did it.

Why you can't win (the theorem)

Any plan is 4 bits, so there are only 16 deterministic plans. Checking all of them, none wins more than 3 of the 4 question pairs: 75%. Shared randomness only mixes plans, so it can't beat the best one either. This is Bell's theorem in its CHSH form (1964/1969).

The quantum pair shares one entangled Bell pair. Each player rotates their qubit by an angle that depends only on their own question, then measures. Interference makes their bits agree with probability cos²(π/8) ≈ 85.4%, which no plan reaches. The circuit is H · CX · RY(θx) ⊗ RY(θy): 2 qubits per round.

Magic square

A 3×3 grid of signs. You get a row, your partner gets a column.

Your row must multiply to +. Their column must multiply to −. You win if you agree on the cell where they cross.

Why you can't win (the theorem)

Multiply all 9 numbers by rows and you get +1. Multiply them by columns and you get −1. No grid of numbers can do both, so at least one cell always clashes. The bot checks all 4,096 deterministic plans: the best wins 8/9.

Mermin and Peres (1990) put a two-qubit observable in each cell instead of a number. Each row of observables multiplies to +I and each column to −I, and every observable in a row or column commutes with the others. With two shared Bell pairs, both players read their line in one joint measurement and they always agree: 9/9. Our circuit needs no ancillas: each line gets a 2-qubit Clifford that rotates its observables onto Z, 4 qubits per round.

Zip

Your partner will run a measurement you never get to see. You send them one message beforehand, and they must output strings that score high on a test (linear XEB). Random guessing scores 0.

The quantum player sends n qubits. You send k bits. How many bits do you need to keep up?

This is the 2025 “quantum information supremacy” experiment (Kretschmer, Grewal, …, Aaronson), done on a 12-qubit trapped-ion computer.

Why you can't win (the theorem)

For 12 qubits the paper proves that any classical protocol reaching the hardware's score of 0.427 needs at least 78 bits, and the best known one uses about 330. The quantum message is 12 qubits. The gap grows exponentially with n: the bound is Ω(ε²·2ⁿ). Our impostor is one natural strategy, not the best possible one; the theorem covers them all.

Circuit: Alice's state is a random brickwork of U and RZZ gates with a periodic boundary. The wrap-around gate between the last and first qubits is native on all-to-all trapped-ion hardware and costs SWAPs on a nearest-neighbour chip. Bob applies a random Clifford and measures.

What we do not claim

  • A simulator proves no physical advantage. It computes the correlations. The advantages here are theorems about separated players and about bits, and they hold however fast your computer is.
  • CHSH (1969) and the magic square (1990) are not new. The format is: you play the impostor, there is a /dev/urandom ablation, and the three levels together make one argument.
  • Our Zip impostor is one strategy among many; the 78-bit bound is the paper's.

Everyone sells quantum computing as speed. Speed is the advantage nobody can check today. Correlation, memory and precision can be checked with a pencil. A quantum computer is the thing you can't fake.
